Her work helps ensure that Utah County’s records are accurate, up to date, and useful to the people who rely on them. Since joining the Utah County Assessor’s Office in August 2023, she has brought real estate experience, a strong sense of responsibility, and a collaborative approach to her work.
One of Alys’s major responsibilities is coordinating building permits from 28 jurisdictions throughout the county. In 2025, she received and processed 10,827 permits. For each one, she identifies key information and routes it to the appropriate field appraiser. That work helps the Assessor’s Office track new construction, maintain an accurate tax roll, and account for growth across Utah County.
Alys is also proactive about getting the information the office needs. If permits are missing or a city’s submissions are delayed, she follows up with city personnel and her supervisor to help make sure nothing is overlooked. She is currently working with a data analyst to explore using AI software to extract relevant information from permits more efficiently.
Her attention to detail benefits colleagues across the county, too. A GIS Division employee who maintains address points used in the 911 system praised Alys for entering clean, accurate address information and keeping them informed about changes. When questions or errors come up, Alys is helpful and solutions-oriented, either resolving the issue herself or connecting the employee with someone who can.
Alongside these responsibilities, Alys assists residents at the front counter and answers incoming calls. Colleagues describe her as pleasant, dependable, and easy to work with. She brings a sense of humor to the office, while staying focused when it’s time to get the work done.
Much of Alys’s contribution happens behind the scenes, but its impact is clear. Her diligence supports accurate records, responsive service, and the important work of the Assessor’s Office. NOTICE IS HEREBY GIVEN pursuant to the provisions of the Local Government Bonding Act, Title 11, Chapter 14, Utah Code Annotated 1953, as amended (the “Act”), that on October 7, 2026, the Board of County Commissioners (the “Commission”) of Utah County, Utah (the “Issuer”), adopted a resolution (the “Resolution”) in which it authorized the issuance of the Issuer’s Sales Tax Revenue Bonds, Series 2026 (the “Series 2026 Bonds”) (to be issued in one or more series and with such other series or title designation(s) as may be determined by the Issuer), and called a public hearing to receive input from the public with respect to (a) the issuance of the Series 2026 Bonds and (b) any potential economic impact that the Series 2026 Project (defined below) financed with the proceeds of the Series 2026 Bonds may have on the private sector.
TIME, PLACE AND LOCATION OF PUBLIC HEARING
The Issuer shall hold a public hearing on November 4, 2026, at the hour of 2:00 p.m. at 100 East Center Street, Room 1400, Provo, Utah.  The purpose of the hearing is to receive input from the public with respect to (a) the issuance of the Series 2026 Bonds and (b) any potential economic impact that the Series 2026 Project (defined below) to be financed with the proceeds of the Series 2026 Bonds may have on the private sector.  All members of the public are invited to attend and participate.
PURPOSE FOR ISSUING THE SERIES 2026 BONDS
The Series 2026 Bonds will be issued for the purpose of (a) financing the construction of a new public works facility and related improvements (the “Series 2026 Project”), (b) funding any debt service reserve funds, as necessary, and (c) paying costs of issuance of the Series 2026 Bonds.
PARAMETERS OF THE SERIES 2026 BONDS
The Issuer intends to issue the Series 2026 Bonds in the aggregate principal amount of not more than Thirty Million Dollars ($30,000,000), to mature in not more than twenty-one (21) years from their date or dates, to be sold at a price not less than ninety-eight percent (98%) of the total principal amount thereof and bearing interest at a rate or rates not to exceed six and one half percent (6.50%).  The Series 2026 Bonds are to be issued and sold by the Issuer pursuant to the Resolution, including as part of said Resolution, a General Indenture of Trust and a Supplemental Indenture of Trust (together, the “Indenture”) which were before the Commission in substantially final form at the time of the adoption of the Resolution and said Indenture is to be executed by the Issuer in such form and with such changes thereto as shall be approved by the Issuer; provided that the principal amount, interest rate or rates, maturity, and discount of the Series 2026 Bonds will not exceed the maximums set forth above.  The Issuer reserves the right to not issue the Series 2026 Bonds for any reason and at any time up to the issuance of the Series 2026 Bonds.
SALES AND USE TAXES PROPOSED TO BE PLEDGED
The Issuer proposes to pledge all of the revenues produced by the sales and use taxes levied by the Issuer under the County Option Sales and Use Tax revenues received by the Issuer pursuant to Title 59, Chapter 12, Part 11, Utah Code Annotated 1953, as amended, for the Series 2026 Bonds and all or any portion of other sales or excise taxes legally available to be pledged to the Series 2026 Bonds (the “Revenues”).
OUTSTANDING BONDS SECURED BY PLEDGED TAXES
The Issuer currently has $420,000 of bonds outstanding secured by the Revenues.
OTHER OUTSTANDING BONDS OF THE ISSUER
Additional information regarding the Issuer’s outstanding bonds may be found in the Issuer’s financial report (the “Financial Report”) at:  http://secure.utah.gov/auditor-search/.  For additional information, including any information more recent than as of the date of the Financial Report, please contact the County Auditor at (801) 851-8110.
TOTAL ESTIMATED COST OF BONDS
Based on the Issuer’s current plan of finance and a current estimate of interest rates, the total principal and interest cost of the Series 2026 Bonds to be issued under the Act to finance the Series 2026 Project, if held until maturity, is $37,561,157.

It’s an incredible opportunity to improve healthcare for Utah County.”Data CentersOctober 7, 2026UTAH COUNTY, Utah — A proposed ordinance in Utah County would create tight restrictions on any potential data-center development, aiming to protect ratepayers, water resources and the environment while preserving private property rights. The ordinance, still awaiting the Planning Commission’s recommendation, would require compliance with those development restrictions along with the holding of two public meetings and other steps before a data-center permit application could be filed.
Utah County Commissioner Amelia Powers Gardner said data centers are not new to the county. Bluffdale’s NSA facility was among the first, and in the Eagle Mountain area, Meta has had a presence with an agreement in place for more than five years and QTS has operated there for some time. She noted that Utah County has been studying data-center projects for several years and has sought to learn from existing deployments elsewhere.
“We want to make sure that any impact to infrastructure is paid for by the business,” said Gardner. “We want to make sure we’re not negatively affecting our citizens. And we want to protect the environment for future generations.”
The proposed ordinance would only apply for data centers proposed to be located in the unincorporated areas of Utah County and would require extensive plans for and consideration of potential impacts, including environmental, air quality, noise, ground water, and power and water consumption. The county has been examining what standards and protections should be mandated by county ordinance when a proposed data center seeks to come to the area, and the process has benefited from input from the public.  There are no current application requests for any proposed data centers in the unincorporated area of Utah County. “In the transit and transportation world, that is the distance from your main transit hub,” Utah County Commissioner Skyler Beltran said, explaining the term last mile. “How do we get people from those locations to their home or to the service that they are using or to their job, which is typically within a couple miles? And this connects them from the main hub to where they need to go?”
The expansion targets Eagle Mountain, Saratoga Springs, Lehi and a portion of American Fork, with the aim of easing congestion as the county’s population swells. Beltran described the plan as a proactive step to begin building ridership for UTA and public transit in a region where traffic and gridlock are persistent issues.
“This on-demand service will allow you in Eagle Mountain, Saratoga, Lehi to go within six miles,” Beltran said, adding that there is no cap if you travel to the Lehi or American Fork FrontRunner stations, which links riders into the broader network.
Officials emphasize that UTA Connect is not intended to compete with private ride-hailing services. Beltran highlighted several differentiators: a network that keeps trips within the UTA system and a shared-ride model that pools riders headed in similar directions.
“We do not want to compete with the private sector; there are key differences—the network connection within the UTA system and the shared ride,” he said.
Riders will pay $2.50 per trip, with service available within two hours of a ride request. The fare allows trips from city centers to FrontRunner stations or across the network, and trips can be requested through the On Demand UTA app, which will indicate the driver’s arrival time and availability.
“It is two dollars and fifty cents,” Beltran said, with a note that the app makes the service simple and intuitive to hail and track.
The move comes as Utah County anticipates continued rapid growth. Beltran noted that Eagle Mountain was the ninth-fastest growing city in the United States in 2025, and the expanded service area will reach about 180,000 people across Saratoga Springs, Lehi and Eagle Mountain, all while roughly $1.5 billion in road projects proceed concurrently.

Provo, UT (June 8, 2026) – Utah County Clerk Aaron Davidson will host a free public Town Hall on Thursday, June 11 at 7:00 PM in the Utah County Commissioners Chambers to address a growing concern: a significant number of first‑time voter ballots are not being counted. With the June 23 primary election approaching and the voter registration deadline this Friday, Clerk Davidson is urging voters—especially young voters—to take simple steps now to ensure their ballots are counted.
When a mail‑in ballot envelope is missing a signature or the signature does not match what is on file, the ballot is flagged for the signature‑cure process. Election officials then contact the voter to verify the signature. The system protects voters, but only works when voters respond quickly. Many do not see the notice or miss the deadline, resulting in valid ballots going uncounted.
Young voters are disproportionately affected. In the 2024 General Election, voters ages 18–25 accounted for 55.46% of all ballots that were challenged and not cured. Among voters ages 18–21, 13.46% of all ballots cast were challenged—roughly one in seven. These ballots are legitimate votes from eligible Utahns who participated but were blocked by a preventable issue.
“Every eligible vote deserves to be counted,” said Clerk Davidson. “Young voters are showing up, but small technical issues are standing between them and having their voice heard. With the primary approaching, now is the time to act.”
The June 11 Town Hall will explain how to check ballot status, how the curing process works, and what voters can do immediately to ensure their ballot is counted. The event is free, open to the public, and requires no registration. Voters ages 18–25 and their parents are especially encouraged to attend.
Location: Utah County Commissioners Chambers, 100 East Center Street, Provo, Utah 84606. For those unable to attend in person, the Town Hall will also be livestreamed: Utah County Elections - YouTube.
The Utah County Clerk’s Office administers elections for more than 371,000 registered voters and is committed to transparent, accessible, and accurate elections. For information on ballot status or curing, contact the Utah County Elections Division.

Provo, Utah — The Utah Lake Authority is launching a new media initiative called “HABs Happen” to better educate the public about harmful algal blooms (HABs). The campaign aims to equip residents and visitors with clear information and practical tools to understand what HABs are and what they are not.
Utah County Commissioner Skyler Beltran attended the media announcement and offered strong support for ongoing habitat and water quality work around Utah Lake.  “While algae is a natural part of lake ecosystems, it’s important to recognize the significant progress that has been made in recent years to mitigate it. In 2025, efforts to reduce phragmites and restore native vegetation achieved more progress than ever before. Those efforts are making a measurable difference and Utah Lake is now in its best condition in many years.”
What HABs are and why they matter
HABs are a naturally occurring cyanobacteria found in every body of water in Utah and throughout the United States. Their appearance can range from green pea soup to globules that resemble grass clippings. While ingesting HABs can cause illness, serious health effects are rare. HABs can be toxic for dogs, which should avoid contact with contaminated water. The campaign emphasizes that HABs are not an emergency every year, but rather a predictable seasonal occurrence that can be anticipated and managed with information and preparation.
HABs Season – Know before you go
Harmful algae blooms happen every year and are predictable
They are more likely to be at the end of summer when it’s hot
They are mildly dangerous to humans but toxic to dogs
Commissioner Beltran wants Utahns to be mindful, take precautions but have fun when going outdoors.  “Utah Lake remains safe for recreational use, and I hope to see residents and visitors out enjoying it this summer.”
Starting in late July to early August, consistently hot temperatures create conditions for HABs to develop. While localized blooms can occur earlier in the year, the campaign encourages a “know-before-you-go” mindset—similar to precautions taken by skiers during avalanche season or hikers in the narrows. The public is encouraged to visit habs.utah.gov to see where HABs may be, and to avoid areas with blooms.
